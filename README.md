<h1>Helping Hands – Assistive Grip System</h1>


<h2>Description</h2>

Helping Hands is an assistive engineering device developed by a five-person
team at Colorado School of Mines to help individuals with reduced grip strength
use everyday handled objects more easily.

The final design uses a wearable magnetic interface with interchangeable
attachments to transfer part of an object's load from the user's grip to the
hand and wrist while maintaining an easy-to-use attachment and release system.

My primary technical responsibility was the design and validation of the
magnetic handle-attachment subsystem. I also contributed to stakeholder
research, concept development, system integration, prototyping, and testing.

<h2>Engineering Design Process</h2>

<h3>1. Problem Definition & Stakeholder Research</h3>

Our team investigated how individuals with reduced grip strength interact
with everyday handled objects.

My stakeholder research included interviews with older adults and an
occupational therapist specializing in hand therapy. In addition I researched some studies and simulated
restricted hand dexterity (fig 1.) to better understand common difficulties with gripping and manipulating everyday objects.

<h3></h3>

Overall, after engaging with stakeholders and reviewing research, hand accessibility issues are centered around grip strength, pinch strength, leverage, and the ability to complete simple daily tasks without pain or frustration. The problem is not just severe injury or visible deformity, but the everyday difficulty of opening jars, holding utensils correctly, maintaining grip on objects, and applying force at the right angle. Small tasks become inefficient, tiring, or even impossible when strength and dexterity are limited. Moving forward, any solution we developed must focus on reducing required grip force, increasing leverage, improving surface contact, while remaining simple and intuitive to use. If we were to address those core needs, we would be directly targeting the real challenges of those individuals with hand accessibility limitations.

<h3>3. Magnetic Handle-Attachment Subsystem</h3>

My primary subsystem focused on creating the magnetic interface between the
glove and tool attachment.

Design requirements included:

- Support loads up to approximately 15 lb
- Maintain a secure connection during use
- Minimize unwanted rotation
- Remain compact
- Allow users with reduced grip strength to intentionally disconnect the tool

I explored multiple methods of integrating a neodymium magnet into a
3D-printed component, including an exposed magnet slot (fig. 6) and a fully enclosed
magnet pocket (fig. 7).

<h3>4. Engineering Analysis</h3>

The subsystem was evaluated against a 15-lb load requirement.

15 lb × 4.45 N/lb ≈ 67 N

The analysis compared this requirement with the available magnetic force
while accounting for expected losses caused by material between the
magnetic surfaces. This helped determine the magnetic configuration required for the
subsystem and informed integration with the glove attachment. The ideal case would be 3-D printing with a filament with metal infused in it that the magnets on the glove could attach to, but unfortunately this is not an option as the special filaments we have access to, while metal infused, would not be affected by magnets. 


<h3>5. System Integration & Final Prototype</h3>

The final device combined the magnetic interface with a compression glove
and interchangeable grip attachments.
Our team coordinated dimensions, magnet placement, holding-force
requirements, materials, and attachment geometry so the independently
developed subsystems functioned as one system.
